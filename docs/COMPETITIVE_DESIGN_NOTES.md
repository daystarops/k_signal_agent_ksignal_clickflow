# K-Signal Competitive Design Notes

## Summary

K-Signal should combine NYT-like hierarchy and trust, BuzzFeed’s instinct for immediately legible formats, and Vice’s specific, tension-rich headline energy. The result should stay smaller, calmer, bilingual, and unmistakably K-Signal: a clear reading surface that translates Korean internet context without copying any reference publication.

## NYT Lessons: Layout and Trust

- Borrow the obvious hierarchy: one lead story, a clearly subordinate second story, then compact supporting stories.
- Use rules, spacing, restrained color, and stable typographic roles to make editorial judgment visible.
- Keep date and issue context quiet but findable; they help the homepage feel published rather than merely populated.
- Group stories by importance and relationship, not by making every card equal.
- Let supporting stories sit close enough to the lead to read as an editorial package.
- Preserve dense coverage only when navigation and rhythm can support it; K-Signal’s four-story issue benefits from more air.
- Borrow footer maturity—basic publication identity, privacy, accessibility, and terms—but not NYT’s corporate scale.
- Do not copy the masthead, exact grids, typography, or newspaper ornament.

## BuzzFeed Lessons: Shareability and What to Avoid

- Borrow immediate format recognition: a reader should know whether an item is a reported signal, guide, list, or future quiz before opening it.
- Headlines work when they promise a specific payoff and sound like a human noticed something.
- Category labels can reduce hesitation when they remain short and consistent.
- Cards should contain one dominant reason to click; image, label, headline, and dek should not all compete.
- Social packaging benefits from self-contained hooks, but the public article title must remain accurate.
- The observed homepage was image- and card-heavy, with many long curiosity headlines and commercial modules; this creates substantial visual noise.
- Avoid stacked promotional bands, repeated bright treatments, emoji as structural decoration, and endless equal-weight modules.
- Avoid making K-Signal feel like a shopping feed or forcing every cultural observation into a listicle.

## Vice Lessons: Tone and Click Appetite

- Specific nouns and strange-but-true details create appetite faster than generic importance claims.
- Lead with the tension: who expected what, what changed, and why the mismatch matters.
- A headline can have attitude without accusing people of motives the reporting cannot prove.
- Use confident verbs and concrete stakes; avoid timid institutional phrasing.
- Package culture as lived and consequential, not as a detached trend report.
- Keep K-Signal mature by attributing fan claims, preserving uncertainty, and avoiding humiliation as entertainment.
- Vice’s compact category navigation and recognizable publication footer feel mature; its account, security, and fulfillment links reflect infrastructure K-Signal does not have.
- Do not copy Vice’s all-caps aggression, visual collisions, sensational edge, or broad entertainment churn.

## Footer / Publication Architecture Notes

K-Signal needs About, Contact, Privacy, Cookie Settings, Accessibility, and Terms now, plus a quiet copyright line. Cookie Settings should land on the honest cookie section until a preference UI exists.

Login, Sign Up, Security Policy, Fulfillment Policy, Refund Policy, Advertise, and Careers should wait for real accounts, payments, sponsorship operations, or company processes. As K-Signal grows, the footer can add editorial standards, corrections, newsletters, contributor information, and only then business or account links backed by actual systems.

## K-Signal Recommendations

- **Homepage layout:** Keep the current lead/secondary/supporting hierarchy. Add future story volume through named packages or archives, not an undifferentiated card wall.
- **Nav:** Keep lanes compact and bilingual. Let labels route to useful archives before adding more categories.
- **Search:** Preserve the direct search control, Korean/English matching, lane filter, and uncluttered result rows.
- **Footer:** Use small type, a thin navy boundary, a subtle red rule, six publication links, and a quiet copyright line.
- **Article pages:** Protect reading width, visible receipts, translation pairing, correction access, and restrained related-story packaging.
- **Headline style:** State a concrete tension with a strong verb; be sharper than corporate and more clickable than academic, while attributing claims and protecting nuance.
- **Mobile:** Stack editorial priority cleanly, shorten chrome, keep tap targets usable, and avoid horizontal carousels as the main discovery model.
- **Future category/archive pages:** Use a clear lane title, one featured signal, chronological cards, search/filter support, and bilingual context without explaining the whole taxonomy on every screen.

## Do Not Do

- BuzzFeed-style visual clutter.
- Fake login or sign-up links.
- A giant corporate footer before the organization needs one.
- Over-explaining categories on the homepage.
- Copying NYT’s visual language too closely.
- Becoming Vice-style chaos.
- Losing the Korean/English cultural bridge.
- Turning attributed online reactions into claims about what “Koreans think.”
